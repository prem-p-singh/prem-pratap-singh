Machine learning has moved from a novelty in plant pathology to a default method. The interesting question is not whether that happened, but what the field pointed it at.

I pulled the publication record from OpenAlex and matched it against FAO world production data. The adoption story is real. The targeting story is harder to defend.

## AI genuinely took over, and this is not just publishing growth

Raw paper counts are a weak measure, because scientific publishing has grown enormously on its own. A count that rises 13-fold might mean nothing. The honest measure is the share: of everything published on plant disease, how much involves AI?

![Line chart showing AI's share of plant disease papers rising from about 3 percent in 2005 to about 15 percent in 2025](/data/ai-attention-vs-crop-importance/ai_share_over_time.png)

<Takeaway>
AI's share of plant disease research rose from 3.1% in 2005 to 14.8% in 2025. Roughly one paper in seven now involves machine learning, up from one in thirty.
</Takeaway>

That result survives the normalisation, which is what makes it worth stating. The growth is not an artefact of more journals and more papers overall. The field really has reorganised around these methods within about a decade.

## Then it pointed them at tomatoes

If AI research followed agricultural importance, the crops that feed the most people would attract the most work. They do not.

![Scatter plot of world production against AI plant-disease papers, showing tomato high despite modest production and sugarcane low despite the largest production](/data/ai-attention-vs-crop-importance/attention_vs_production.png)

<Takeaway>
Tomato is the single most studied crop in AI plant-disease research, with 2,848 papers, more than rice, maize, or wheat. Sugarcane, the world's largest crop by tonnage, has 443.
</Takeaway>

Dividing attention by production makes the gap explicit.

![Bar chart of AI plant-disease papers per million tonnes produced, led by coffee, tomato, apple, orange and grape, with sugarcane, barley and cassava at the bottom](/data/ai-attention-vs-crop-importance/papers_per_tonne.png)

<Takeaway>
Tomato receives 11.5 times more AI disease research per tonne grown than maize. Cassava, a staple for hundreds of millions of people, gets 0.9 papers per million tonnes. Sugarcane gets 0.2.
</Takeaway>

## Why tomatoes, and why it matters

Part of this is defensible. Crops differ in value per tonne, and a tonne of coffee is worth far more than a tonne of sugarcane, so some attention premium on high-value horticulture is rational. Coffee topping the per-tonne ranking is not by itself a scandal.

Tomato is harder to explain that way. The most likely driver is not agronomic importance but data availability. The public image datasets that made plant disease a convenient deep-learning benchmark are dominated by a small number of crops, with tomato especially well represented. Once a labelled dataset exists, it becomes cheap to publish another model on it, and the literature compounds around the data rather than around the problem.

That mechanism is worth naming plainly: **research attention follows the datasets, and the datasets were not built to reflect what the world grows.**

The consequence lands where it hurts most. Cassava, sugarcane, and barley sit at the bottom of the per-tonne ranking, and cassava in particular is a food-security staple across sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. These are also crops where field conditions are harder, labelled images are scarcer, and a working diagnostic tool would arguably do the most good.

## What this analysis does not show

It counts papers, not quality, and certainly not deployment. A crop with many papers may still have no tool a grower can use, and a crop with few papers may be well served by conventional diagnostics that never needed AI. Keyword matching also captures the literature imperfectly, since a study can be about a crop without naming it in the abstract.

The comparison is also one-dimensional. Production tonnage is a reasonable proxy for scale but a poor proxy for value, calorie contribution, or number of people dependent on the crop. Weighting by economic value would flatter the horticultural crops, and weighting by dietary dependence would sharpen the cassava gap further.

What the analysis does support is narrower and still uncomfortable. The distribution of AI plant-disease research is not explained by how much of each crop the world grows, and the crops furthest from the spotlight are disproportionately staples.

The follow-up I would want is a direct test of the dataset hypothesis: catalogue the public plant-disease image datasets by crop, then check whether per-crop paper counts track dataset availability more closely than they track production. If they do, the fix is not to exhort researchers to care about cassava. It is to build the cassava dataset.
