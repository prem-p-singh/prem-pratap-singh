Two things go wrong at the same time, and you need to know which one caused the harm.

There is a method for exactly this. It takes an effect you can measure and splits it in two: the part that travels **through** something in the middle, and the part that goes around it.

## The problem I had

Grapevine Red Blotch Virus damages wine grapes, and hot seasons make that damage worse. The question was why.

Two explanations were possible, and they call for opposite responses:

1. Heat makes the virus multiply faster, and the extra virus harms the vine. **Then you manage the virus.**
2. Heat harms the vine directly, and the virus is just along for the ride. **Then you manage the heat.**

Measuring temperature, virus levels and the vine's response separately cannot tell these apart. You need a method that asks how much of one effect passes through the other.

## How the method works

The idea is simpler than the name suggests.

First, I check how strongly heat moves the middle thing, which here is the amount of virus in the vine. Then I check how much that virus level explains the vine's response, once heat is already accounted for. Multiply those two together and you get the part of the effect that travels through the virus. Whatever is left over is the part that does not.

One practical detail matters. That "multiply two things together" step makes the uncertainty lopsided, so the usual error bars do not apply. Instead, I re-ran the whole calculation on 2,000 resampled copies of the data and took the range of answers. It is a slower way to get a confidence interval, and a more honest one here.

## What it answered

Across the genes I tested, 39 showed heat acting through the virus after correcting for the fact that testing thousands of genes will throw up false positives by chance.

The strongest was a gene the vine needs in order to switch on its heat defences. **About 62% of heat's effect on that gene travelled through the virus.**

That number changed how I read the whole system. The virus is not sitting beside the heat damage. It carries most of it, and it does so by interfering with the machinery the vine would otherwise use to cope with heat. On this reading, the vine does not fail because the heat is too strong. It fails because the infection stops it responding to heat normally.

I should be careful here. This is a strong statistical signal in one system, and it still needs testing at the bench before anyone treats it as settled.

## The same method, somewhere completely different

If a method only works on the data it was built for, it is not much of a method. So here is the identical calculation pointed at public farming data.

The world grows far more food now than in 1961. Two things could explain that: **more farmland**, or **more food from the same farmland**. The difference matters, because clearing new land usually costs habitat while raising yield does not.

That is the same shape of question:

| | Grapevine study | Farming data |
|---|---|---|
| What starts it | Season temperature | Passing years |
| The middle thing | Virus level in the vine | Area of farmland |
| What comes out | The vine's response | Food produced |

![Dot plot showing the share of production growth that came through more farmland, for six crops, with uncertainty ranges](/methods/causal-mediation/proportion_mediated.png)

<Takeaway>
Sugarcane grew almost entirely by taking more land: 97% of its production increase ran through farmland expansion. Rice is the opposite at 13%. Maize sits in between at 73%.
</Takeaway>

Rice is worth pausing on. World rice production has roughly quadrupled since 1961 with only a modest increase in land, which is why its share comes out so low. That is the Green Revolution showing up in the arithmetic.

## Where the method breaks

Four crops returned a share outside 0 to 100%, which cannot be a share of anything.

My first guess was that the maths was struggling because years and farmland move together too closely to separate. **That guess was wrong.** Sugarcane's two measures track each other almost perfectly and the answer is fine; tomato's track each other just as closely and the answer is not. So I looked at what the two quantities were actually doing.

![Scatter plot of farmland growth against production growth since 1961, showing three patterns](/methods/causal-mediation/growth_regimes.png)

<Takeaway>
Wheat, potato and barley now use less farmland than in 1961 while producing more food. Their share goes negative because the land route works against the total rather than with it.
</Takeaway>

Three real patterns sit behind those numbers:

- **Land shrank and yield did the work.** Wheat, potato, barley. More food from less ground, which is the best outcome on that chart.
- **Land and yield both contributed.** Sugarcane, maize, cassava, banana, rice. The share means what it appears to mean.
- **Land grew faster than output.** Soybean and tomato, where farmland expanded so much that the yield side turns negative once land is accounted for.

Reporting these rather than quietly dropping them is the point. A share that lands outside its own limits is the method telling you the summary number no longer applies, and the honest response is to show what the underlying quantities are doing instead.

## What this does not claim

Years are not a treatment anyone assigned, so this splits up an observed trend rather than proving cause. The method also assumes nothing unmeasured is driving both the middle thing and the outcome, and in the farming case that assumption plainly fails, since fertiliser, irrigation and breeding all changed over the same period. World totals also hide a great deal, because a crop expanding on one continent and retreating on another appears here as one blended number.

The grapevine version rests on firmer ground, since temperature there varied across a controlled sampling design rather than drifting along with history.

What I take from both is the same. This method tells you how an effect divides, once you have argued on other grounds for which thing comes first. It will never supply that ordering for you, and the interesting question in the farming case is whether the pattern holds when you break the world totals apart by region.
