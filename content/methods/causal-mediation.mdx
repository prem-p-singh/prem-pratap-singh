A hot summer makes red blotch virus much worse for wine grapes. Everyone in the field knows this.

Nobody could say why.

## The problem

There are two suspects, and they point to opposite solutions.

**Suspect one: the heat feeds the virus.** Warm weather lets the virus multiply, and the extra virus damages the vine. If this is right, you go after the virus.

**Suspect two: the heat hurts the vine directly.** The virus is present but not really involved. If this is right, you go after the heat, with shade, irrigation, or different sites.

You cannot tell these apart by measuring temperature, virus levels and vine damage one at a time. All three go up together in a hot year, no matter which suspect is guilty.

## How I worked it out

The method is a blame-splitter. It asks how much of the heat damage travels *through* the virus, and how much goes straight to the vine.

It works in two steps:

1. **Does heat actually raise the virus level?** If hot years do not mean more virus, suspect one is already dead.
2. **Does the virus level explain the vine's damage, once you have already accounted for the heat?** If it does, the virus is genuinely carrying some of the harm.

Multiply those two steps together and you get the share of the damage running through the virus. Whatever is left is the heat acting alone.

I also needed to know how solid that number was. So I re-ran the entire calculation 2,000 times, each time on a slightly reshuffled version of the data, and looked at how much the answer moved. If it stays put across 2,000 runs, it is probably real.

## The answer

**About 62% of the heat damage travelled through the virus.**

Out of all the genes I tested, 39 showed this pattern clearly. The strongest was a gene the vine uses to switch on its own heat protection.

That flips the story. The virus is not a bystander in a hot year. It is carrying most of the harm, and it does it by jamming the vine's heat defences. The vine is not losing because the summer is too hot. It is losing because the infection stops it from coping with a hot summer.

That said, this is one statistical result in one system. It points the wet-lab work in a clear direction, and it is not settled until that work is done.

## Now try it on farming

A method that only works on the data it was built for is not worth much. So I pointed the same blame-splitter at something completely unrelated.

The world grows far more food than it did in 1961. There are two ways that could have happened: **we farm more land**, or **we get more from each field**. The difference matters, because new farmland usually comes out of forest and habitat.

Same shape of question, different suspects:

| | Grapevine | Farming |
|---|---|---|
| The cause | Hot season | Passing years |
| The go-between | Virus level | Amount of farmland |
| The result | Damage to the vine | Food produced |

![Dot plot showing the share of production growth that came from more farmland, for six crops](/methods/causal-mediation/proportion_mediated.png)

<Takeaway>
Sugarcane grew almost entirely by taking more land, at 97%. Rice barely used extra land at all, at 13%. Maize sits between them at 73%.
</Takeaway>

Rice is the standout. Production has roughly quadrupled since 1961 on only a little more land, which is the Green Revolution showing up in plain arithmetic.

## Where it goes wrong

Four crops came back with a share above 100% or below zero. Neither is possible for a share.

My first guess was that years and farmland move together too closely for the method to separate them. **That guess was wrong.** Sugarcane's two measures track each other almost perfectly and its answer is fine. Tomato's track each other just as closely and its answer is not. So I went and looked at what the numbers were actually doing.

![Scatter plot of farmland growth against production growth since 1961](/methods/causal-mediation/growth_regimes.png)

<Takeaway>
Wheat, potato and barley grow more food today on less land than in 1961. The share goes negative because farmland moved the opposite way to production.
</Takeaway>

Three real situations sit behind those odd numbers:

- **Less land, more food.** Wheat, potato, barley. The best outcome on the chart.
- **Both mattered.** Sugarcane, maize, cassava, banana, rice. Here the share means what it looks like.
- **Land grew faster than food.** Soybean and tomato, where farmland expanded so fast the yield side turns negative.

I report these instead of dropping them because an impossible share is useful information. It is the method saying the single summary number no longer fits, and the right response is to show what the underlying numbers are doing.

## What this does not prove

Nobody assigned the years, so the farming version splits up a trend rather than proving cause. It also assumes nothing unmeasured is pushing both farmland and food output, and that plainly fails here, since fertiliser, irrigation and breeding all changed over the same period. World totals hide a lot too, because a crop growing on one continent and shrinking on another shows up as a single blended number.

The grapevine version stands on firmer ground, since the temperature there varied across a planned sampling design rather than drifting along with history.

What I take from both is the same thing. This method tells you how blame divides between two causes, but only after you have decided, on other grounds, which one comes first. It will never work that ordering out for you. For the farming question, the thing I would check next is whether the pattern still holds once you split the world totals by region.
