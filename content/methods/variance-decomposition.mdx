## The question behind the split

A grapevine block does badly. Was it the season, the virus, or the block itself?

In the vineyard work I answer that per gene, splitting expression variance across
season and infection so I can say which genes respond to the pathogen rather than
to the weather. The arithmetic does not care that the inputs are genes. Any
measurement with more than one label attached can be divided the same way.

So here the labels are crop, country, and year, and the measurement is yield.

## What that actually measured

Crop identity looks overwhelming at 73.9%. It is close to meaningless.

Sugarcane yields tens of thousands of kilograms per hectare. Wheat yields a few
thousand. Putting them in one model and discovering that "crop" explains most of
the spread is close to discovering that sugarcane is not wheat. The number is
real and the interpretation is empty.

This is the failure worth knowing about. A variance decomposition will always
return a tidy set of percentages summing to 100, whether or not the factors mean
anything comparable.

## One crop at a time

Remove crop identity by running the split inside each crop separately. Now the
question is sharper: for the same crop, does yield depend more on where it is
grown or on which year it is?

<CropVarianceRanking />

Across 63 crops the median is 69.1% from country and 5.6% from year. Place beats
year by roughly twelve to one.

Tea is the exception, at 57.9% from year. Its yields climbed steadily for six
decades almost everywhere, so time carries the signal instead of geography.

## Use this when

Use it when you have one measurement and several labels, and you need to know
which label to act on. Variance dominated by country points at agronomy and
infrastructure. Variance dominated by year points at weather or a technology
shift. Variance sitting in the residual says the labels you collected are not
the ones that matter.

## Read the result carefully

The year share here is not a weather signal. Across a 63-year panel it mostly
captures the long climb in yields from breeding and inputs, not the difference
between a good and a bad season. Separating trend from shock needs a different
model.

The panel is balanced on purpose, keeping only country and crop pairs recorded in
every year from 1961 to 2023. That makes the factors comparable, and it also
drops countries and crops with gaps in reporting, which are unlikely to be a
random selection.

Nothing here is causal. Country is a label standing in for soil, climate,
irrigation, cultivars, and policy at once. The split says where the variation
sits, not what to do about it.
