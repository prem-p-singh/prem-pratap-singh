## Start with the decision

A crop performs poorly. Is the problem the season, the location, or something we did not measure?

Variance decomposition shows where variation sits. Here, yield is divided among crop, country, year, and the unexplained remainder.

## The naive split

Pool every crop into one model and ask how much of the variation each label carries.

<YieldVarianceSplit />

## Why the first answer fails

Crop identity appears to explain 73.9%. That number is real, but it is not useful.

Sugarcane and wheat operate on different yield scales. Pooling them mostly proves that they are different crops. A tidy percentage does not guarantee a useful comparison.

## One crop at a time

Run the model separately for each crop. The question becomes actionable: for the same crop, does yield vary more by place or by year?

<CropVarianceRanking />

Across 63 crops, the median split is 69.1% from country and 5.6% from year, about twelve to one.

Tea is the exception: 57.9% comes from year because yields rose steadily across countries.

## Use this when

Use variance decomposition when one outcome has several labels and you need to know where to investigate:

- **Country dominates:** look at agronomy, infrastructure, climate, and policy.
- **Year dominates:** look at weather, breeding, or technology shifts.
- **Residual dominates:** the important factor may not have been measured.

## Keep the limits visible

Year is not the same as weather. Across 63 years, it also captures breeding and better inputs. Separating long-term trend from annual shock needs another model.

The balanced panel keeps only country-crop pairs reported every year from 1961 to 2023. That improves comparability but excludes incomplete reporters.

This is not causal. Country bundles soil, climate, irrigation, cultivars, and policy. The split locates variation; it does not identify its cause.
